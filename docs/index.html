Make it sound professional, it said. Half the time it changed what you meant.

"Keep the meaning exactly the same." Asked to polish contracts, drug labels and everyday messages, small language models changed what the text actually says (a dose, a deadline, a condition, a "shall" that became a "may") in about half of their professional rewrites, and in about 1 in 12 plain grammar fixes. fineprint points you at the edits worth checking.

A tool, a benchmark and a game · by Rishikesh

People hand contracts, prescriptions and emails to chatbots with one instruction: keep the meaning. A diff tool shows every changed comma. fineprint shows only the changes that alter what the text says: numbers and units, dates, negations, obligations (must, shall, may), scope (all, some, only), conditions (if, unless), direction (before, after), who does what, and clauses that silently disappear.

04

Method and limits

Passages. 587 texts people really ask a chatbot to polish: 200 contract clauses (LEDGAR), 200 dosage and warning paragraphs from FDA drug labels (openFDA), and 187 messages where a real user asked ChatGPT to fix their grammar (WildChat-1M and ShareGPT).

Proofreaders. Qwen3-1.7B on all 587 passages; Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma-3-4B on a fixed subset of 100 per domain. Each got two instructions, "Fix the grammar and spelling … do not change its meaning" and "Rewrite … more professional, keep the meaning exactly the same", with greedy decoding on a laptop (MLX, 4-bit).

Detector. A token-level diff (Myers), then each changed span is checked for number values (3 = three, 1,000 = 1000), units counted with synonyms ("per day" = "daily"), negations (don't = do not), modal strength, quantifiers, conditions, direction classes, actors, capitalised names, and dropped clauses. A category only counts if it also differs across the whole sentence, so a word that merely moved isn't flagged. Typo fixes ("subjcet" → "subject") are paired off first.

Gold sets. Each passage edited twice: 2 to 4 harmless proofreading edits (typos, commas, synonyms, contractions, digits to words, British to US spelling), plus one planted meaning change of a known kind in half the pairs. Seed 7 was used for development; seed 11 was held out and run once.

Validation. Version 0.1 was tuned only on Qwen's outputs. A Gemini 3.5 Flash judge then labelled 180 Llama and Gemma proofreads: 105 that fineprint flagged, checking each flag, and 75 it didn't, looking for misses. Version 0.2 compares counts across the whole passage, not just the sentence, and demotes "who does it" changes to minor. It was developed on the Llama labels and measured on the Gemma labels. The headline rates are judge-corrected: flag rate × judge precision + unflagged rate × judge miss rate, with bootstrap 95% CIs.

What the judge found. On real edits from Llama and Gemma, the models it was not tuned on, fineprint rarely misses: only 4 of 75 unflagged edits hid a meaning change. But on "fix the grammar" edits only about 4 in 10 of its flags are real meaning changes (8 of 21 on the held-out model), and on full "professional" rewrites about 1 in 8 (12 of 101). The synthetic gold sets, at 99.8% precision, badly overstated this. Treat fineprint as a highlighter that says where to look, not as a verdict.

Limits. It reads words, not meaning: a paraphrase that changes meaning without touching a number, a negation or a keyword ("the tenant pays" → "the landlord pays" without the word "tenant") can slip by. Rewrites for "professional" tone change so many words that flags pile up; read the judge numbers before trusting a count. English only. The judge is itself a model.
